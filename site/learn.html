Notes for the people who read the COA.

These are the things a formulator, a QA lead or a procurement manager needs in order to judge whether a cordyceps supplier is worth a first order. Farmologic is pre-harvest; what follows is general guidance for assessing any supplier, including us.

Actives

What cordycepin actually is

Cordycepin is 3′-deoxyadenosine, a nucleoside that looks almost exactly like adenosine with one hydroxyl group missing. That single structural difference is why it is of interest.

The commercially important point for a formulator is that Cordyceps militaris — the orange, cultivated species — produces cordycepin in its fruiting body, and can be grown to a specification. Ophiocordyceps sinensis, the wild Himalayan species, is harder to source, and harder to confirm the identity of. If your label claim depends on cordycepin, C. militaris is the species that lets you stand behind the number.

Content is not fixed. It varies with strain, substrate, growing conditions and harvest timing, which is why a single headline figure means less than a result measured on the batch in front of you.

What to ask for: cordycepin as % w/w, assayed by HPLC, on the specific batch you are buying. Adenosine reported alongside it. Anything expressed only as “polysaccharides” or “beta-glucans” is measuring something else entirely. Useful, but not cordycepin.

not the one in the brochure.

Cultivation

Indoor-grown versus wild-collected

There is a romantic argument that wild-collected cordyceps must be superior because nature made it. In a supply contract, that argument falls apart quickly.

Wild material carries the conditions it grew in: the altitude, the season, the host, the collector's handling and the length of the trade chain. Across a supply chain that long, traceability and consistency can be difficult to establish.

Controlled indoor cultivation replaces those unknowns with parameters. Strain is documented. Substrate is built to a recipe from inputs traced to lot. Temperature, humidity, air and light are held to set points and logged. Harvest happens at a defined maturity rather than whenever a collector reached the slope.

The result is not a compromise on wild material. It is what lets us promise a buyer the same result twice, and repeatability is what a product line is actually built on. It also means supply does not depend on wild collection, which matters if sustainability is in your brand story.

The trade-off to know: indoor cultivation carries costs that collection does not. You are paying for control. If your formulation does not need control, you do not need us.

Quality

How to read a cordyceps COA

A certificate of analysis is a claim about one specific batch, made by whoever tested it. Most of the value is in checking that those two things are what you assume them to be.

  1. 1

    Whose laboratory?

    In-house or independent? An independent laboratory is a different proposition from a supplier's own bench, and the report should name it.

  2. 2

    Which batch, which date?

    The batch number must match the material in front of you and the date must be recent. A generic “typical analysis” is not a COA.

  3. 3

    What part of the organism?

    Fruiting body or mycelium on grain? The two are different materials, and only one supports a fruiting-body claim on your label. Mycelium grown on grain is supplied together with its substrate.

  4. 4

    Cordycepin, and by what method?

    HPLC with a stated result as % w/w. If the certificate lists only polysaccharides or beta-glucans, cordycepin was never measured.

  5. 5

    Safety panel complete?

    Heavy metals by ICP-MS, total plate count, yeast and mould, E. coli, Salmonella, and pesticide residue. With limits stated, not just “complies”.

  6. 6

    Moisture and ash

    Unremarkable lines, but worth reading. Moisture tells you the water left in the material; ash tells you the total inorganic residue.

Three questions for any supplier
  1. Can I see the certificates for your last three batches, not a typical analysis?
  2. What is your contamination rate, and what happens to a failed batch?
  3. If a batch comes back out of specification, who bears it, and will you tell me before you ship?
