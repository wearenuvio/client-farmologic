Actives

What cordycepin actually is

Cordycepin is 3′-deoxyadenosine, a nucleoside that looks almost exactly like adenosine with one hydroxyl group missing. That single structural difference is why it is interesting, and it is the compound most cordyceps claims ultimately point back to.

The commercially important fact for a formulator is simple: Cordyceps militaris (the orange one, cultivated) reliably produces cordycepin in its fruiting body. Ophiocordyceps sinensis, the wild Himalayan caterpillar fungus, is scarce, expensive and extremely difficult to verify. If your label claim depends on cordycepin, C. militaris is the species that lets you stand behind the number.

Content is not fixed. It moves with strain, substrate, light cycle and harvest timing, which is precisely why a supplier who quotes a single number without a per-batch assay is telling you about their marketing, not their material.

What to ask for: cordycepin as % w/w, assayed by HPLC, on the specific batch you are buying. Adenosine reported alongside it. Anything expressed only as “polysaccharides” or “beta-glucans” is measuring something else entirely. Useful, but not cordycepin.

The number that matters is the one on your batch,
not the one in the brochure.

Cultivation

Indoor-grown versus wild-collected

There is a romantic argument that wild-collected cordyceps must be superior because nature made it. In a supply contract, that argument falls apart quickly.

Wild material carries the conditions it grew in: the altitude, the season, the host, the collector's handling, the length of the trade chain, and whatever it picked up along the way. Two consignments from the same region can differ substantially in active content and in contaminant load.

Controlled indoor cultivation replaces those unknowns with parameters. Strain is documented. Substrate is built to a recipe from inputs traced to lot. Temperature, humidity, air and light are held to set points and logged. Harvest happens at a defined maturity rather than whenever a collector reached the slope.

The result is not a compromise on wild material. It is what lets us promise a buyer the same result twice, and repeatability is what a product line is actually built on. It also takes commercial pressure off a fragile wild ecosystem, which matters if sustainability is in your brand story.

The trade-off to know: indoor cultivation costs more in capital and energy than collection does in labour. You are paying for control. If your formulation does not need control, you do not need us.

Quality

How to read a cordyceps COA

A certificate of analysis is a claim about one specific batch, made by whoever tested it. Most of the value is in checking that those two things are what you assume them to be.

  1. 1

    Whose laboratory?

    In-house or independent? An accredited third-party laboratory is a different proposition from a supplier's own bench.

  2. 2

    Which batch, which date?

    The batch number must match the material in front of you and the date must be recent. A generic “typical analysis” is not a COA.

  3. 3

    What part of the organism?

    Fruiting body or mycelium on grain? Both are legal; only one lets you claim fruiting-body content. Grain-grown mycelium carries substrate with it.

  4. 4

    Cordycepin, and by what method?

    HPLC with a stated result as % w/w. If the certificate lists only polysaccharides or beta-glucans, cordycepin was never measured.

  5. 5

    Safety panel complete?

    Heavy metals by ICP-MS, total plate count, yeast and mould, E. coli, Salmonella, and pesticide residue. With limits stated, not just “complies”.

  6. 6

    Moisture and ash

    Unremarkable lines that quietly tell you how the material was dried and how much non-fungal matter came along.

Three questions for any supplier
  1. Can I see the certificates for your last three batches, not a typical analysis?
  2. What is your contamination rate, and what happens to a failed batch?
  3. If a batch comes back out of specification, who bears it, and will you tell me before you ship?
